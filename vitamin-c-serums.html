How to choose

Form matters most. L-ascorbic acid is the form with the strongest research behind it. It only gets into skin when the formula is acidic (below pH 3.5), and absorption peaked at 20% in the classic study — so a higher number on the bottle doesn’t automatically mean more benefit. Common derivatives such as magnesium ascorbyl phosphate didn’t raise skin vitamin C levels in that same study.

Freshness matters next. Vitamin C breaks down in air and light. A serum that has turned orange or brown has lost potency. Water-free formulas and mix-before-use formats are two ways brands work around this; storing any vitamin C serum capped, cool and away from light helps too.

Then match it to your skin. The strong, acidic formulas are the likeliest to sting, especially on sensitive or dry skin. If you’re new to vitamin C or have reactive skin, start with a gentler formula, or use a strong one every other day, and patch-test first.

What the science actually says

Absorption: in percutaneous absorption studies, L-ascorbic acid entered the skin only when formulated below pH 3.5, and skin levels peaked at a 20% concentration. Three common derivatives tested — magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, ascorbyl-6-palmitate and dehydroascorbic acid — didn’t raise skin vitamin C levels (Pinnell et al., Dermatologic Surgery, 2001).

The vitamin C + E + ferulic combination: adding ferulic acid to a 15% vitamin C / 1% vitamin E solution improved its chemical stability and roughly doubled its protection of skin against simulated sunlight, from about 4-fold to about 8-fold, measured by redness and sunburn cells (Lin et al., Journal of Investigative Dermatology, 2005). That study was co-authored by the researcher behind the C E Ferulic formula, and it measured protection against UV damage — not wrinkle reduction.

What that means for you: vitamin C is an antioxidant that complements sunscreen; it is not a replacement for it. Claims about fading dark spots or fine lines rest on smaller studies, so treat any serum as a slow, modest improvement in the look of your skin — not a transformation.

Who should take care

Strong, acidic vitamin C serums can sting or redden sensitive, very dry or broken skin. Patch-test on your inner arm or jaw first, avoid the eye area, and stop if irritation persists. If you use prescription skincare (such as a retinoid) or have a skin condition, ask your dermatologist how to fit vitamin C into your routine.

Questions, answered

Morning or night?

Most brands suggest the morning, under sunscreen, since vitamin C’s best-supported role is antioxidant protection during the day.

My serum turned orange. Is it still good?

A deepening orange or brown color means the vitamin C has oxidized and lost potency. Replace it.

Is a higher percentage better?

Not past a point. In the classic absorption study, skin levels peaked at 20%; stronger formulas mainly add the risk of irritation.

Can I use it with retinol?

Many people use vitamin C in the morning and retinol at night to keep irritation down. If your skin is sensitive, introduce one product at a time.
