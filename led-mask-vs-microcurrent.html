LED mask or microcurrent? What each one actually does

Two of the most popular at-home devices — a red light therapy face mask and a microcurrent facial toning device — work in completely different ways. Compiled from published brand documentation, user manuals and the research — we haven’t run our own hands-on tests; see how we choose.

The short version

Choose an LED mask if…

You want a hands-free routine: strap it on for a few minutes and get on with your evening. Red light is aimed at the look of fine lines and overall complexion; blue light at the look of blemishes. Our pick: Dr. Dennis Gross SpectraLite FaceWare Pro. Price tier: $$$.

SpectraLite FaceWare Pro: the hands-free pick

A flexible, cordless LED mask from a dermatologist-founded brand. You choose red mode, blue mode, or both, and each session ends on its own after three minutes, which makes it the easiest of the two to keep up. The brand lists 100 red and 60 blue LEDs (its own pages aren’t entirely consistent on the count) and includes a USB charger, an adjustable head strap and a storage bag.

The trade-offs: it does nothing for the look of lift, and like any LED device the results build slowly and depend on sticking with it.

NuFACE Trinity: the contouring pick

The best-known at-home microcurrent device. Two rounded probes pass a gentle current over a layer of conductive gel while you glide the device upward along the jaw, cheeks and forehead. The manual calls for about five minutes per session, five days a week for the first 60 days, then two to three times a week to maintain.

The trade-offs: it’s a hands-on habit, every session needs gel, and the routine matters — the brand describes the effect as needing regular maintenance. Attachments, including an LED wrinkle attachment, are sold separately.

What the science actually says

Red light: the evidence is promising but modest. In one controlled trial, 136 volunteers given twice-weekly red-light sessions reported improved skin complexion and showed increased collagen density on ultrasound compared with controls (Wunsch & Matuschka, 2014). That trial used clinical light units rather than a home mask, so treat home results as gentler and slower. Terms like wavelength and irradiance are explained in our science glossary.

Microcurrent: the published evidence is thinner — mostly small studies, many of them run or funded by device makers. Any visible change is cosmetic and temporary without regular sessions.

Neither device treats a medical condition, and neither replaces sunscreen, which remains the best-supported step for the look of aging skin. Red light therapy gets its own deep dive in our upcoming Episode 2.

Who should skip these

Microcurrent: NuFACE’s manual says not to use its devices if you are pregnant, have a pacemaker or other implanted electronic device, have epilepsy or a seizure disorder, have active cancer, have had recent facial surgery, or are under 18.

LED masks: talk to your doctor or dermatologist first if you take medication that makes you sensitive to light, have a light-sensitive condition, or have an eye condition. Follow the manual’s guidance on eye safety.

Questions, answered

Can I use both?

They work differently, and many people pair them. Follow each manual, and don’t buy both at once — start with the one that matches your main goal and the routine you’ll actually keep.

Which is faster?

Per session, the LED mask: three hands-free minutes versus about five hands-on minutes plus gel.

Does “FDA-cleared” mean it’s proven to work?

No. FDA clearance means the device was found substantially equivalent to a device already on the market for its stated use. It isn’t the same as FDA approval, and it doesn’t promise a particular result.

How long until I see a difference?

Both brands frame results in weeks of consistent use, not days. If you won’t keep up the routine, neither device is worth the money.
