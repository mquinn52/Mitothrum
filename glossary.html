Science glossary

The science terms in our guides, defined

Short definitions of the terms you’ll meet in light-therapy and infrared-sauna product pages, with the source for every claim. Numbers that come from a brand are labelled as the brand’s.

Light therapy (photobiomodulation)

Wavelength

The color of light a device emits, measured in nanometres (nm). Red light sits at roughly 600–700 nm; near-infrared starts just above 700 nm and can’t be seen. In one 2014 controlled trial of 136 volunteers, twice-weekly red light (611–650 nm) improved measured skin roughness and collagen density compared with untreated controls, and adding near-infrared showed no extra benefit[1]. That trial used clinical light units rather than a home mask, so treat home results as gentler and slower.

Irradiance

How much light power reaches each square centimetre of skin, measured in milliwatts per square centimetre (mW/cm²). When a product page lists it, the number is the manufacturer’s, measured at a distance the manufacturer chose.

Before you use light therapy: talk to your doctor or dermatologist first if you take medication that makes you sensitive to light, have a light-sensitive condition, or have an eye condition. Compare devices in our LED mask vs. microcurrent guide.

Electromagnetic fields (EMF)

Magnetic field (mG, µT)

Magnetic field strength is given in milligauss (mG) or microtesla (µT); 1 µT equals 10 mG. The international guideline body ICNIRP sets a reference level of 200 µT (2,000 mG) for general-public exposure to 50 and 60 Hz magnetic fields[2].

Electric field (V/m)

The strength of the electric field around a plugged-in or operating device, measured in volts per metre (V/m). ICNIRP’s general-public reference level is 5 kV/m at 50 Hz and about 4.2 kV/m at 60 Hz, the US mains frequency[2].

Reading an EMF claim

When a brand advertises “low EMF,” look for a test report that names the lab, the meter, and the distance each reading was taken at. A number with no distance can’t be compared with anything. We report brand figures as the brand’s, and we don’t call any device “safe.”

Infrared heat and saunas

Far infrared (IR-C)

ICNIRP classes infrared from 3 µm to 1 mm as IR-C, or far infrared. In far-infrared cabins, heat exchange with the body is almost purely radiative, and the cabin air may be about 40 °C or less[3].

Radiant vs. convective heat

Radiant heat travels as infrared and warms the skin it reaches; convective heat warms the air, which then warms you, as in a traditional hot-air sauna. Because far-infrared cabins add almost no hot-air heating, they have to deliver more radiant heat to warm the body as much as a conventional sauna[3].

Before you use one: don’t use an infrared sauna or sauna blanket if you’re pregnant, have cardiovascular conditions or heat sensitivity, or have implanted devices — talk to your doctor first. Compare options in our sauna blanket comparison.
